# Approaches to idea generation

Index for selection. For a chosen approach open its method cards in `methods/<id>.md`.
Sources and confidence: `PROVENANCE.md`.

## a1 · Jobs to Be Done (JTBD)

Start from the goal for which a person “hires” a product: functional, social or emotional, rather than from demographics. The main question: what job does the person hire the product to do? The ways of collecting data (interviews, observation, support requests) were chosen for the project; the JTBD schools (Christensen, Ulwick, Moesta) have their own techniques.

- Why combine with checking: A user-side axis of assessment and a cheap experiment are needed: first make sure the pain is real, and only then build.
- Check steps this approach helps: 1. Hypothesis; 5. Assessment: three axes; 6. Mini-experiment
- Approach source: https://www.wikiwand.com/en/articles/Jobs_to_Be_Done
- Methods: g8 Design thinking; g14 QFD and FCA; i3 How Might We; n1 Problem finding; n5 James Lind Alliance; n8 Grounded theory

## a2 · Analogies and transfer

Take a solution from another field (biology, transport, games, medicine) and adapt it. For example, move the mechanics of one game genre into another.

- Why combine with checking: Check that the transfer works in the new place: what already exists, and whether the technology and economics fit.
- Check steps this approach helps: 4. Desk research; 5. Assessment: three axes
- Approach source: https://groups.psych.northwestern.edu/gentner/papers/Gentner83.2b.pdf
- Methods: p5 Associative theory; p6 Bisociation (secondary link); p7 Structure mapping; p12 Random stimuli (secondary link); g5 Biomimetics; j1 NM method (Nakayama); q1 Synectics; d4 Fixation on examples; d5 Distant and near analogies

## a3 · SCAMPER

Substitute, combine, adapt, modify, put to another use, eliminate, reverse. Seven questions to ask of an existing solution (Eberle, 1971, based on Osborn's list).

- Why combine with checking: Every transformation carries assumptions. Write them down and check them against what others have already done.
- Check steps this approach helps: 2. Assumptions and risks; 4. Desk research
- Approach source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SCAMPER
- Methods: g3 SIT; g4 Morphological analysis; g6 Focal object method (secondary link); i10 Attribute listing

## a4 · Constraints

What if it were half the price, had no interface, took one minute? Hard limits cut off the usual answers and push toward new ones. Not every constraint helps: the useful ones are those that demand effort, and expertise in the field is needed.

- Why combine with checking: The constraint itself becomes a success criterion. Then check technical feasibility.
- Check steps this approach helps: 3. Success criteria; 5. Assessment: three axes
- Approach source: https://scottbarrykaufman.com/does-creativity-require-constraints
- Methods: g1 TRIZ; g3 SIT (secondary link); j4 Extenics (Cai Wen)

## a5 · First principles

Break the problem down into basic facts and build the solution anew, without relying on “how it is usually done”.

- Why combine with checking: The assumptions are written out explicitly. What remains is to assess the technical and economic axes.
- Check steps this approach helps: 2. Assumptions and risks; 5. Assessment: three axes
- Approach source: https://concepts.dsebastien.net/concept/first-principles/
- Methods: g1 TRIZ (secondary link); g2 ARIZ; g7 C-K theory; i7 Axiomatic design

## a6 · Gap analysis

Study the market, articles and reviews of competitors. Look for unmet demand and for what has only just become technically possible.

- Why combine with checking: Confirm that the gap is real, is not closed by analogues, and that people are ready to pay for or use the solution.
- Check steps this approach helps: 4. Desk research; 5. Assessment: three axes
- Approach source: https://askattest.com/blog/articles/how-to-find-gaps-in-the-market
- Methods: p10 Abduction (secondary link); p13 Serendipity; g9 Blue ocean strategy; n1 Problem finding (secondary link); n2 Problematization instead of gap-spotting (secondary link); n3 Problem selection (Fischbach, Walsh); n4 Evidence gap maps; n5 James Lind Alliance (secondary link); n6 Horizon scanning (secondary link); n7 Literature-based discovery; n8 Grounded theory (secondary link); n9 Anomalies (Kuhn) (secondary link); n13 AI hypothesis generation; n15 Hamming questions

## a7 · Divergence → convergence

Quantity first: brainstorming, brainwriting, “100 ideas in 20 minutes”. Then selection against criteria set in advance. The same logic is in the Double Diamond model (Design Council, 2005): widening and narrowing first for the problem, then for the solution.

- Why combine with checking: Selection follows the criteria. Rank by risk and set success thresholds in advance.
- Check steps this approach helps: 2. Assumptions and risks; 3. Success criteria
- Approach source: https://www.scrum.org/resources/blog/double-diamond-uk-design-council
- Methods: p1 Wallas stages; p2 Geneplore; p4 Divergent thinking; p11 Incubation and insight; g13 Mind maps; q3 Brainwriting 6-3-5 (secondary link); q4 Nominal group technique (secondary link); i1 Creative Problem Solving; i6 KJ method; i12 Disney method; d2 Alone first, then as a group (secondary link); d4 Fixation on examples (secondary link); d6 AI and idea diversity (secondary link)

## a8 · Extreme users

Study those who use the product in non-standard ways: they may reveal needs hidden in the typical scenario, and their solutions often help ordinary users as well. Risk: drifting away from the target market.

- Why combine with checking: The sample is small. Watch for measurement bias and do not extend the conclusions to everyone.
- Check steps this approach helps: 6. Mini-experiment; 7. Measurement
- Approach source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extreme_users
- Methods: g8 Design thinking (secondary link); g10 Lead users; i5 Cultural probes; n14 Crowdsourcing and citizen science

## a9 · Brainstorming

Osborn's method: more ideas, criticism is deferred, wild ideas are welcome, ideas are combined. But in a review of 22 studies (Diehl, Stroebe), groups usually produced fewer ideas than the same people working alone: waiting for one's turn to speak gets in the way. “Nominal groups” work better: ideas are written separately, then collected.

- Why combine with checking: Many ideas result, so selection is needed: rank by risk and set criteria in advance, otherwise the loudest one gets chosen.
- Check steps this approach helps: 2. Assumptions and risks; 3. Success criteria
- Approach source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brainstorming
- Methods: p4 Divergent thinking (secondary link); q1 Synectics (secondary link); q2 Six Thinking Hats; q3 Brainwriting 6-3-5; q4 Nominal group technique; i8 Lotus blossom; d1 Production blocking; d2 Alone first, then as a group; d3 Debate vs “don't criticize”; d6 AI and idea diversity

## a10 · Wild ideas (70/20/10)

Deliberately include a share of “crazy” ideas. A common template: about 70% feasible, 20% bold and 10% that sound idiotic at first. Among the latter there are breakthroughs; the rest teach to think more broadly. Related techniques: Edward de Bono's provocation (Po), that is, making a statement to see where it leads, the “worst idea”, reverse brainstorming. No independent confirmation that the 70/20/10 proportion is the best was found; the original article did not open.

- Why combine with checking: A wild idea must not be dropped on first impression. Write down the assumptions and check them with a cheap experiment.
- Check steps this approach helps: 2. Assumptions and risks; 6. Mini-experiment
- Approach source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Po_(lateral_thinking)
- Methods: p12 Random stimuli; i2 Provocation and movement (PO)

## a11 · Assumption reversal

Write down the “obvious” assumptions about the problem and deliberately reverse each one. What if it is the opposite? What if the user pays for exactly the opposite?

- Why combine with checking: The reversed assumption immediately becomes a testable hypothesis, and measurement will show which of the versions is right.
- Check steps this approach helps: 2. Assumptions and risks; 7. Measurement
- Approach source: https://www.creativitypost.com/article/turn_your_assumptions_upside_down
- Methods: p3 Types of creativity (Boden); p9 Janusian thinking; p10 Abduction; g7 C-K theory (secondary link); g9 Blue ocean strategy (secondary link); j4 Extenics (Cai Wen) (secondary link); n2 Problematization instead of gap-spotting; n9 Anomalies (Kuhn); n10 Counterfactual thinking; n11 Thought experiments

## a12 · Change of scale

Look at the problem across different orders of magnitude of a chosen scale (size, time, cost, complexity): add or remove a zero and look for patterns that repeat at different levels (the Powers of Ten technique). For example, one user or a million, a minute or a year.

- Why combine with checking: An idea may work on a small scale and break on a large one, or the other way round. Assess the economics at both scales, run the pilot at the small one.
- Check steps this approach helps: 5. Assessment: three axes; 6. Mini-experiment
- Approach source: https://hpi-epic.github.io/dt-at-it-toolbox/methods/08%20-%20Powers%20of%20Ten.pdf
- Methods: none

## a13 · Method merging

Combine techniques from two different fields into one. Example: game mechanics in financial education. This also covers the transfer of methods between disciplines. This is one author's practical view, not a proven regularity: the same author notes that a set of methods does not replace the creative leap itself.

- Why combine with checking: Check that the junction is really new and that the two parts are compatible technically and economically.
- Check steps this approach helps: 4. Desk research; 5. Assessment: three axes
- Approach source: https://opus.cloud1.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/144963
- Methods: p3 Types of creativity (Boden) (secondary link); p5 Associative theory (secondary link); p6 Bisociation; p8 Conceptual blending; i11 Forced connections; g4 Morphological analysis (secondary link); g6 Focal object method; i10 Attribute listing (secondary link); j3 SECI (Nonaka, Takeuchi); n7 Literature-based discovery (secondary link); n13 AI hypothesis generation (secondary link)

## a14 · Speculating about new technologies

Imagine different futures: probable, plausible, possible and preferable (Dunne, Raby). What becomes possible when the technology matures, and which ideas will pay off there?

- Why combine with checking: Rely on verifiable trends, not on expectations. Make sure the technology already exists at least in prototypes, and assess the technical axis.
- Check steps this approach helps: 4. Desk research; 5. Assessment: three axes
- Approach source: https://strangehorizons.com/non-fiction/articles/the-art-of-futures/
- Methods: g11 Backcasting; g12 Scenario planning; i4 Speculative design; q5 Delphi method; n6 Horizon scanning
